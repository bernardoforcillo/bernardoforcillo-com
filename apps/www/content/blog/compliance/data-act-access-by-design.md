**Key takeaways**

- Under Article 3(1) of the Data Act, connected products and related services
  placed on the market **after 12 September 2026** must be designed so that
  product data, related service data and the metadata needed to use them are
  accessible to the user **by default, easily, securely, free of charge**, in a
  comprehensive, structured, commonly used, machine-readable format, and
  **directly** where relevant and technically feasible.
- The rule is **not retroactive**: products placed on the market earlier do not
  have to be redesigned. Users' right to request their data has applied since
  12 September 2025.
- **Micro and small enterprises are exempt** from the connected-product chapter,
  unless they have a larger partner or linked enterprise or make the product
  as a subcontractor. Medium-sized enterprises get one year.
- The word **"securely"** connects the two laws: a data access path is attack
  surface, and the Cyber Resilience Act's Annex I applies to it from December
  2027.
- Build the access path once, through the same threat model, release process
  and documentation as the rest of the product.
- **Italy** had not designated a Data Act authority or set penalties as of mid 2026, but users' rights apply anyway, the Garante is competent where personal data is involved, and other Member States have enforcers.

## Two September deadlines, one product

September 2026 brought two obligations for makers of connected products, one day
apart. On 11 September, the CRA's duty to report actively exploited
vulnerabilities began. On 12 September, the Data Act's access-by-design rule
began to apply to connected products and related services placed on the market
from that date. Faegre Drinker described them together as the two September
deadlines for connected-product manufacturers, and that is how they land in
practice: on the same device, the same app and the same cloud.

They also pull in a way that looks contradictory at first. The Cyber Resilience
Act (CRA) wants a small attack surface and data minimisation. The Data Act wants
users to reach all the data their product generates, ideally directly. The two
are reconciled at design time, and this article is about how.

I build connected products at Ganiga, where the device generates data its
owners want and a cloud back end processes it, so both laws are on our table. I
am not a lawyer; the sources are at the end.

## What Article 3 asks

The Data Act (Regulation (EU) 2023/2854) has applied since 12 September 2025.
Article 3(1), deferred by Article 50, applies to connected products and related
services placed on the market after 12 September 2026. The Commission's Data Act FAQ applies this unit by unit, as EU product law does: a unit of an older model placed on the market after that date has to comply. It requires them to be
designed, manufactured and provided so that data is accessible to the user
under a set of qualifiers, each of which has design consequences.

| Qualifier                                  | What it means for design                                                                                       |
| ------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Product data and related service data      | Data the product generates about its use or environment, and data generated by the digital services it needs |
| Including relevant metadata                | Enough context to interpret and use the data: units, timestamps, schemas, sensor identifiers                    |
| By default                                 | Access is available without the user having to negotiate or ask specially                                     |
| Easily                                     | No undue technical or procedural hurdles                                                                       |
| Securely                                   | Only the right user gets the data, intact and confidential                                                     |
| Free of charge                             | No fee to the user for access                                                                                  |
| Comprehensive, structured, commonly used, machine-readable | A documented format, not screenshots or PDFs                                                    |
| Directly, where relevant and technically feasible | The product's design lets the user reach the data without going through you                            |

Three limits keep the obligation proportionate. It covers data that is readily
available to the manufacturer through the product's design, not data you would
have to build new pipelines to obtain. Derived or inferred data, the output of
your own analysis, is outside it, as Matheson notes. And the qualifier "where
relevant and technically feasible" applies to direct access: according to
Matheson's reading of the Commission's FAQ, manufacturers keep some discretion
over whether to design for direct access, but the data must then be available
indirectly.

Article 3 also has an information duty that has applied since September 2025.
Before a sale, rental or lease, the user must be told what data the product can generate (Article 3(2); the duty falls on the seller, renter or lessor, which may be a distributor rather than you, so give them the data sheet), in what format and volume, whether continuously and in real time,
whether it is stored on the device or on a server and for how long, and how the
user can access, retrieve or erase it. Related services carry a similar duty.
In practice this is a data sheet, and it should come from the same source as
your technical documentation.

## Direct or indirect access

The Data Act distinguishes two routes, and most products end up using both.

```tikz Direct and indirect access to product data, and where the CRA applies.

Where direct access is not feasible, the user requests data from the back end, which is Article 4 indirect access. At the user's request, the back end shares data with a third party under Article 5. Every path needs the same controls from the CRA's Annex I: authenticate the user, authorise per device, encrypt, limit rate and log access.

**Direct access** means the product's own design lets the user access, stream
or download the data: a local interface, an export function, an API the user
controls. **Indirect access** under Article 4 means the user asks the data
holder, often the manufacturer, which must then make readily available data and
metadata accessible without undue delay, with the same qualities. Under Article
5, the user can also ask for the data to be shared with a third party of their
choice.

A useful first question for each product is: who is the user? The Data Act's
user is whoever owns the product or has contractual rights to use it, such as a
renter or lessee, or receives a related service. For a connected bin owned by a
waste operator, the user is the operator, not the citizen who throws rubbish in
it. For a shared device in a household or a fleet, there may be several users,
and the access design has to separate them.

## Who is in scope, and who is exempt

A connected product, for the Data Act, obtains, generates or collects data about
its use or environment and can communicate it, by network, cable or on-device
access. Products whose main function is storing, processing or transmitting
data for others, such as servers or routers, are generally outside, according
to Matheson. A related service is a digital service connected to the product so
that, without it, the product could not perform one or more of its functions.

Article 7 exempts micro and small enterprises from the whole connected-product
chapter, Articles 3 to 6, for products they manufacture or design and related
services they provide. The exemption does not apply if the enterprise has a
partner or linked enterprise that is not micro or small, or if it was
subcontracted to manufacture or design the product. Medium-sized enterprises are
exempt in their first year at that size, and for each product during its first
year on the market. Check the group structure before relying on it: a start-up
majority-owned by a larger company may not qualify.

The exemption does not change the CRA, which applies regardless of size.

## The security side: an access path is attack surface

The CRA's Annex I applies to every product with digital elements placed on the market from 11 December 2027. The data access path falls under it as far as it is part of that product: the interface on the device, the companion app, and a cloud back end only if it counts as the product's remote data processing, meaning you designed it or had it developed under your responsibility and the product needs it for one of its functions. Once data has been shared with a third party under Article 5, that party's systems are outside your CRA scope; the token you issue and the endpoint that serves it are not. This is how I map the two.

| Data Act requirement                         | CRA Annex I property                                  | Design consequence                                                                                       |
| -------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Accessible "securely"                        | (d) access control, (e) confidentiality, (f) integrity | Authenticate the user, authorise per device and per user, encrypt in transit, sign exports if integrity matters |
| Direct access on the device                  | (b) secure by default, (j) limited attack surface      | No unauthenticated local endpoints; access enabled after onboarding, not in the factory state            |
| Continuous or real-time access where relevant | (h) availability, (i) not harming other devices        | Rate limits and back-pressure so data access cannot degrade essential functions                         |
| Sharing with third parties at user request   | (d) access control, (l) logging                        | Scoped, revocable tokens; a record of who was granted what and when                                     |
| Information on how to erase data             | (m) secure erase                                       | The same erase function serves both laws                                                                 |
| Comprehensive data                           | (g) data minimisation                                  | Decide what the product collects under the CRA and GDPR first; then make all of it accessible          |

The last row resolves the apparent conflict. Data minimisation decides what the
product collects in the first place. The Data Act then decides who can reach
what was collected. A product that collects less has less to expose; a product
that collects a lot has to give its users access to a lot, securely.

The Data Act also lets the data holder apply technical protection measures
against unauthorised access or use, as long as they do not hinder users' rights
(Article 11), and protects trade secrets through confidentiality measures and,
in exceptional cases, refusal (Article 4). Article 4(2) adds a narrow security exception: the user and the data holder can agree by contract to restrict access or further sharing where it could undermine the product's security requirements laid down by law and seriously harm people's health, safety or security. Article 4 also limits the data holder: it can ask only for the information needed to check that the requester is the user, it can use the product's readily available non-personal data only on the basis of a contract with the user, and it can share non-personal product data with third parties, for commercial or non-commercial purposes, only to fulfil that contract. Those are tools and limits for the security design, not reasons to make access hard.

Where the data includes personal data, the GDPR still applies to it, and the
access design has to separate users of a shared device. That part is
privacy-by-design work, and it belongs in the same threat model.

## Building it once

The cheapest way I know to meet both laws is to treat the data path as one
feature with one owner:

1. **A data catalogue per product**: every signal the device and services
   generate, with format, frequency, retention and metadata. It feeds the Data
   Act pre-contract information, the CRA's product description and the GDPR
   records.
2. **One export interface** with a documented, commonly used format such as
   JSON or CSV with a published schema, available to the user by default.
3. **Authentication and authorisation tied to ownership**, including transfer of
   ownership and resale, which is when a previous owner's access must end.
4. **Third-party sharing through scoped, revocable grants**, logged.
5. **The interface in the threat model, the SBOM and the release process**, like
   any other part of the product. The [CRA engineering guide](/blog/security/cra-engineering)
   covers how to make that evidence a build output.
6. **Tests for the qualifiers**: can a user get all their data, free, in the
   documented format, without contacting support?

## Enforcement and what is still moving

Each Member State has to designate authorities and set penalties under Articles
37 and 40. In Italy, according to iSimply, none of the European delegation laws
from 2023 to 2025 included a mandate for the Data Act, and as of July 2026 no
national authority or penalty regime had been set. That does not leave the field empty. Where personal data is involved, data protection authorities are already competent for the Data Act (Article 37(3)) and can fine under the GDPR's rules (Article 40(4)), so in Italy the Garante can act today. Nor does it suspend the regulation: users' rights apply directly, and products sold in other Member
States face their enforcers. Germany's parliament, for example, adopted its implementing act (the Data Act Implementation Act, DADG) in March 2026; it has been in force since 30 May 2026, with the Federal Network Agency as the main authority.

The Commission's Digital Omnibus proposal of November 2025 would amend parts of
the Data Act, such as the trade-secret refusal in Article 4(8). As published, it
would not change Articles 3, 7, 37 or 50, according to EU Representa. The
Council agreed its mandate on 21 October 2026; nothing is adopted yet.

## Frequently asked questions

### What does Article 3(1) of the Data Act require?

Connected products and related services placed on the market after 12 September
2026 must be designed so that product data, related service data and the
metadata needed to interpret them are accessible to the user by default,
easily, securely and free of charge, in a comprehensive, structured, commonly
used and machine-readable format, and directly accessible where relevant and
technically feasible.

### Does access by design apply to products already on the market?

No. Article 3(1) applies to connected products and related services placed on
the market after 12 September 2026, so earlier products do not have to be
redesigned. Users of those products have still been able to request their data
from the data holder since 12 September 2025.

### Which companies are exempt from the Data Act's connected-product obligations?

Under Article 7, micro and small enterprises are exempt from Articles 3 to 6 for
products they make or design and services they provide, unless they have a
partner or linked enterprise that is not micro or small, or were subcontracted
to make or design the product. Medium-sized enterprises are exempt during their
first year at that size and for each product's first year on the market.

### What is the difference between direct and indirect access under the Data Act?

Direct access means the product's design lets the user access, stream or
download data without going through the data holder, for example through a
local interface or export. Indirect access, under Article 4, means the user
requests the data from the data holder, which must provide it without undue
delay, free of charge and in a machine-readable format.

### Does the Data Act require a specific data format or API?

No. It requires a comprehensive, structured, commonly used and machine-readable
format but does not prescribe a file format or interface. A documented export in
a common format with a published schema is the usual way to meet it.

### How does the Data Act relate to the Cyber Resilience Act?

They regulate different things on the same product. The Data Act decides who
can access the data a connected product generates; the CRA decides how secure
the product must be. Because the Data Act requires access to be secure, the data
access path is part of the attack surface that the CRA's Annex I requirements
apply to from 11 December 2027.

### Who enforces the Data Act in Italy?

As of mid 2026, Italy had not designated a national Data Act authority or set penalties, according to published analysis. Where personal data is involved, the Garante per la protezione dei dati personali is already competent under Article 37(3). The regulation still applies
directly, users' rights are enforceable, and products sold in other Member States
face those countries' authorities.
